I will be conducting an interview for a Market Data squad lead role. The candidate has already went through screening interview, programming interview, and architecture interview. This one will be leadership. We work with many other domains/teams, where they are dependent on our data. We work as a squad of 5 to 6 people, where you have a squad lead paired together with product manager, and a group of engineers. The current engineers in market data are a mix of seniority from very junior just out of uni, to having some experience across multiple companies. I need a set of questions to follow and ask during the interview so I can evaluate them
Below is a practical 60–75 minute leadership interview guide tailored to your Market Data squad lead role. It focuses on the signals you cannot easily assess in the prior programming and architecture rounds: leading a mixed-seniority squad, partnering with product, serving dependent teams, managing delivery and incidents, and building a healthy engineering culture. Behavioral questions work best when you probe for the candidate’s specific actions, trade-offs, results, and reflection rather than accepting general principles.^1_1^1_3

Aim to select one primary question per section, then use the follow-ups to distinguish a strong answer from a rehearsed one. STAR-style probing is useful, but for a squad lead you should keep pulling the answer back to what they personally did as a leader, not only what the team achieved.^1_3^1_5
Opening context
Use this framing so the candidate can respond concretely:
“Our Market Data squad is typically five to six engineers, paired with a product manager. We serve multiple internal domains that depend on our data, so reliability, clear contracts, timely delivery, and stakeholder trust matter as much as individual technical excellence. The team includes engineers from graduate/junior level through to experienced engineers. In this conversation, I’m interested in how you lead people, partner with product and other teams, and create the conditions for the squad to succeed.”
Then ask:
“Based on that context, what do you see as the three most important responsibilities of the squad lead?”
Listen for:
Creating clarity of mission, priorities, and ownership
Enabling engineers rather than becoming the single technical bottleneck
Building trust with product, dependent teams, and senior stakeholders
Developing people and protecting delivery quality
Managing operational risk and incident response
Follow-up: “Which of those three would you personally spend the most time on in your first 90 days, and why?”
Leadership philosophy
Primary question
“Tell me about your leadership philosophy. How has it changed as you have taken on more responsibility?”
Follow-ups
“Give me a specific example where you applied that philosophy.”
“What did you deliberately stop doing as you became more senior?”
“When have you been wrong about your approach, and what changed afterwards?”
Strong signals
A clear, practical philosophy rather than buzzwords
Evidence of adapting style to context and individuals
Comfort discussing failure, feedback, and learning
Awareness that leadership is measured through team outcomes, growth, and sustainable delivery
Concerns
“My job is to make all technical decisions”
“I lead by being the strongest engineer”
Vague claims of empowerment with no examples
No reflection or evidence of changed behaviour
Engineering-leadership interview guidance consistently highlights people management, coaching, conflict resolution, delivery, and stakeholder management as core evaluation areas.^1_2^1_1
Mixed-seniority squad
This is especially important for your team: a graduate engineer, a mid-level engineer, and a senior engineer need very different support.
Primary question
“Tell me about a time you led a team with a wide range of experience levels. How did you ensure junior engineers grew while senior engineers remained challenged and engaged?”
Follow-ups
“Walk me through one specific junior engineer you developed. What was their starting point, what did you do, and what changed?”
“How did you avoid making seniors carry all the difficult work while juniors only did low-risk tasks?”
“How did you tailor feedback and delegation to each person?”
“What would you do differently now?”
Strong signals
Intentional pairing, mentoring, ownership ladders, and graduated responsibility
Creates safe opportunities for juniors to make meaningful contributions
Uses seniors as mentors and technical leaders, not just delivery capacity
Gives specific, timely, behaviour-based feedback
Tracks growth through concrete outcomes, not tenure
Concerns
“Juniors need to figure it out themselves”
Seniors are treated only as a resource to unblock others
No examples of a person’s actual development
Delegation without support or follow-up
Common EM interview material specifically probes mentoring, pairing senior and junior engineers, individual development plans, and supporting engineers whose growth direction is unclear.^1_7^1_3
Coaching and performance
Primary question
“Tell me about a time an engineer was not performing at the level needed. How did you identify it, what did you do, and what was the outcome?”
Follow-ups
“What was the first conversation like, and what specifically did you say?”
“How did you separate a skill gap, motivation issue, unclear expectations, and personal circumstances?”
“What support did you put in place, and how did you measure progress?”
“If they did not improve, what would your next steps have been?”
“How did you protect the rest of the team during this period?”
Strong signals
Early, direct, compassionate, and specific feedback
Clear expectations and measurable improvement plan
Involves HR/process appropriately where needed
Balances support for the individual with fairness to the team
Can describe both a successful recovery and a difficult escalation
Concerns
Avoidance until the issue becomes severe
Feedback is vague or personal rather than behavioural
No accountability or follow-up
Treats performance management as purely an HR process
Delegation and ownership
Primary question
“Describe a piece of work you delegated that you initially felt you could do better yourself. How did you decide who would own it, and how did you support them?”
Follow-ups
“What decision rights did you give them, and which decisions did you retain?”
“Where did they struggle, and how did you intervene?”
“What did you learn about your own leadership?”
“How do you decide when to step in versus let someone struggle productively?”
Strong signals
Delegates outcomes and context, not just tasks
Matches work to development goals as well as current capability
Sets check-ins, guardrails, and escalation paths
Lets people make reversible mistakes and learn
Steps in early when risk, wellbeing, or delivery is genuinely at stake
Concerns
Delegates only low-risk work
Micromanages implementation details
Uses delegation as a way to avoid accountability
Cannot explain the balance between autonomy and support
Product partnership
Primary question
“Tell me about a time you and your product manager disagreed about scope, priority, or timeline. How did you resolve it?”
Follow-ups
“What was the business need behind the product manager’s position?”
“What engineering constraints or risks were you trying to protect?”
“What options did you bring to the discussion?”
“What compromise or decision was reached, and how did you communicate it?”
“What would you have done if the disagreement could not be resolved at your level?”
Strong signals
Treats product as a partner, not an adversary
Understands customer/business value and translates it into engineering trade-offs
Brings options: reduce scope, phase delivery, add capacity, accept risk, change sequence
Makes risks and consequences explicit
Escalates constructively when needed
Concerns
“Engineering always loses to product”
“I just tell product what is possible”
No curiosity about commercial or customer impact
Avoids difficult conversations or escalates prematurely
Cross-functional alignment, competing priorities, and stakeholder conflict are recurring themes in engineering-manager interview guidance.^1_6^1_1
Delivery and planning
Primary question
“Tell me about a significant initiative you led where the original plan was no longer achievable. How did you recognise it, and what did you change?”
Follow-ups
“What early signals told you the plan was at risk?”
“How did you distinguish an execution issue from a planning or scope issue?”
“What did you communicate to the team, product, and stakeholders?”
“What trade-offs did you make, and who approved them?”
“What did you change permanently in how the squad plans work?”
Strong signals
Regular inspection of progress, risks, dependencies, and quality
Early course correction rather than heroic recovery
Transparent communication, including bad news
Scope, sequencing, quality, and staffing trade-offs made explicitly
A durable process improvement after the event
Concerns
Relies on overtime or individual heroics
Hides risk until the deadline is missed
Blames individuals rather than examining the system
Cannot articulate the actual outcome and lessons
Cross-domain dependencies
For Market Data, this is likely the highest-value section. The candidate needs to show they can lead a platform/data team whose consumers have real operational and commercial needs.
Primary question
“Tell me about a time another team depended on your team’s data, service, or platform and there was a conflict over requirements, timelines, quality, or ownership. How did you handle it?”
Follow-ups
“Who were the stakeholders, and what did each of them need?”
“How did you establish what ‘good’ meant: correctness, freshness, latency, coverage, availability, or supportability?”
“How did you make ownership, contracts, and escalation paths clear?”
“What trade-off did you make, and how did you communicate it?”
“What would you do if a senior stakeholder insisted on an unrealistic commitment?”
Strong signals
Understands consumers as customers with concrete needs
Separates requirements from proposed solutions
Establishes explicit service expectations, data contracts, ownership, and support boundaries
Builds alignment through documentation, reviews, and shared decision-making
Can say no while offering a credible alternative
Concerns
Treats other teams as interruptions
No mechanism for managing requests or expectations
Promises delivery without understanding downstream impact
Avoids difficult stakeholder conversations
This maps directly to your environment: market data consumers will care about data correctness, completeness, timeliness, latency, coverage, schema stability, and incident communication. Strong platform leadership requires explicit ownership and contract boundaries rather than informal coordination alone.
Stakeholder communication
Primary question
“Describe a time you had to communicate an unwelcome update to senior stakeholders—for example, a delay, a quality issue, a data incident, or a change in scope. How did you approach it?”
Follow-ups
“What did you know, what did you not yet know, and how did you communicate that distinction?”
“What options and recommendation did you bring?”
“How did you tailor the message for engineers, product, and senior leadership?”
“What was the reaction, and what did you do next?”
“What would you change if you faced the same situation again?”
Strong signals
Leads with impact, facts, options, and a recommendation
Distinguishes known facts from assumptions
Gives a realistic recovery plan rather than false reassurance
Maintains trust through consistency and follow-through
Adapts communication depth to the audience
Concerns
Delays communication until forced
Presents problems without options or ownership
Overpromises recovery
Blames individuals or vendors without examining systemic causes
Incident leadership
Primary question
“Tell me about a significant production incident involving a system your team owned or depended on. What was your role, and what did you do during and after the incident?”
Follow-ups
“How did you establish severity, impact, and the immediate response?”
“How did you divide roles between investigation, mitigation, communication, and decision-making?”
“What trade-offs did you make between speed of mitigation and root-cause understanding?”
“How did you support the people involved, particularly if they were junior or under pressure?”
“What changed afterwards as a result of the post-incident review?”
Strong signals
Calm, structured incident command
Prioritises user impact, mitigation, and clear communication
Delegates clearly and avoids making one person the single point of failure
Conducts blameless learning and turns findings into concrete actions
Follows through on preventive work
Concerns
Focuses only on technical root cause
No communication plan or stakeholder management
Individual blame rather than systemic learning
No lasting operational improvements
Culture and psychological safety
Primary question
“What do you deliberately do to create a team where people can raise concerns, disagree, admit mistakes, and ask for help?”
Follow-ups
“Give me an example of a time someone challenged your decision or pointed out a mistake you made.”
“How did you respond in the moment?”
“How do you ensure quieter or junior engineers are heard in planning and design discussions?”
“How do you handle a dominant senior voice that shuts down discussion?”
“What rituals or norms have you introduced that made this real rather than aspirational?”
Strong signals
Models vulnerability and receives feedback openly
Creates structured opportunities for input from all levels
Addresses exclusionary or dismissive behaviour directly
Uses retrospectives, design reviews, post-incident reviews, and 1:1s intentionally
Builds norms that survive busy periods
Concerns
“My door is always open” with no supporting mechanisms
Avoids conflict or lets senior voices dominate
Treats psychological safety as avoiding accountability
No examples of changing their own behaviour
Scenario questions
Use one or two of these if the candidate’s examples are thin or you want to test judgement in your specific context.
Competing consumer demands
“Two major dependent teams need changes to the market data platform in the same quarter. Team A needs a new asset class with a hard commercial deadline. Team B needs a reliability improvement because they are experiencing data-quality issues that affect clients. Your squad has capacity for only one major initiative. How would you approach the decision?”
Probe for:
Clarifying business impact, risk, and urgency
Consulting product, consumers, and engineering
Breaking work into smaller increments or temporary mitigations
Making an explicit, communicated trade-off
Avoiding silent deprioritisation
Junior engineer mistake
“A junior engineer deploys a change that causes incorrect prices to be published to a downstream consumer for an hour. The incident is resolved, but the consuming team is frustrated. How do you handle the immediate aftermath, the conversation with the engineer, and the follow-up with the consumer?”
Probe for:
Separating accountability from blame
Supporting the engineer while ensuring process improvements
Transparent stakeholder communication
Concrete prevention: tests, validation, rollout safeguards, alerting, runbooks
Avoiding a response that discourages future ownership
Product pressure
“Your product manager wants to commit to a client-facing feature date, but engineering has discovered that a vendor data feed has inconsistent reference data and requires significant normalisation work. The PM says the commercial commitment cannot move. What do you do?”
Probe for:
Understanding the actual client commitment and consequences
Quantifying risk and effort
Identifying phased delivery, manual controls, reduced scope, or alternative data sources
Presenting a recommendation rather than merely escalating
Maintaining a constructive product partnership

What strong looks like
The strongest candidate will not necessarily have managed the largest team. Look for someone who can:
Explain a clear leadership philosophy and demonstrate it through real situations.
Develop junior engineers without treating senior engineers as unpaid mentors or delivery capacity.
Partner constructively with product while protecting quality, reliability, and sustainable pace.
Manage dependent teams through explicit ownership, contracts, expectations, and communication.
Lead calmly through incidents and convert them into systemic improvements.
Be candid about mistakes, feedback, and what they would do differently.
Show that their impact is visible in team outcomes: delivery, quality, retention, growth, stakeholder trust, and operational resilience.
For your particular squad, I would weight cross-domain stakeholder leadership, mixed-seniority people development, and incident/delivery leadership most heavily. Those are the areas where a technically excellent candidate can still struggle as a squad lead in a market-data platform team serving many consumers.
